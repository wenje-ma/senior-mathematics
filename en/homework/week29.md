> **Definition 4.1.1** (n-th Root) <br>In general, if $x^n=a$ ($n>1$, $n\in\mathbb N^*$), then $x$ is called the $n$-th root of $a$.

> **Definition 4.1.2** (Fractional Exponents) <br>We define $a^{\frac{m}{n}}=\sqrt[n]{a^m}$ ($a>0$, $m,n\in\mathbb N^*$, $n>1$), $a^{-\frac{m}{n}}=\frac{1}{a^{\frac{m}{n}}}$ ($a>0$, $m,n\in\mathbb N^*$, $n>1$).

> **Common Conclusion 4.1.3** (Properties of n-th Roots) <br>When $n$ is odd, $\sqrt[n]{a^n}=a$; when $n$ is even, $\sqrt[n]{a^n}=\left|a\right|$.

> **Common Conclusion 4.1.4** (Operational Properties of Fractional Exponents) <br>$a^r a^s=a^{r+s}$, $(a^r)^s=a^{rs}$, $(ab)^r=a^r b^r$ ($a>0$, $b>0$, $r,s\in\mathbb Q$).

> **Definition 4.1.5** (Irrational Exponents) <br>When $a>0$ and $\alpha$ is an irrational number, $a^\alpha$ is a definite real number.

> **Common Conclusion 4.1.6** (Operational Properties of Real Exponents) <br>$a^r a^s=a^{r+s}$, $(a^r)^s=a^{rs}$, $(ab)^r=a^r b^r$ ($a>0$, $b>0$, $r,s\in\mathbb R$).

> **Definition 4.2.1** (Exponential Function) <br>In general, the function $y=a^x$ ($a>0$, and $a\neq 1$) is called an exponential function.

> **Common Conclusion 4.2.2** (Graphs and Properties of Exponential Functions) <br>The domain is $\mathbb R$, the range is $(0,+\infty)$, and the graph passes through the fixed point $(0,1)$; it is an increasing function when $a>1$, and a decreasing function when $0<a<1$.

> **Common Conclusion 4.2.3** (Exponential Growth and Decay Models) <br>$y=a(1+r)^x$ ($a>0$, $r>0$) is an exponential growth model, and $y=a(1-r)^x$ ($a>0$, $0<r<1$) is an exponential decay model.

> **Definition 4.3.1** (Logarithm) <br>In general, if $a^x=N$ ($a>0$, and $a\neq 1$), then the number $x$ is called the logarithm of $N$ with base $a$, denoted $x=\log_a N$.

> **Definition 4.3.2** (Common Logarithm and Natural Logarithm) <br>The logarithm with base $10$ is called the common logarithm, denoted $\lg N$; the logarithm with base $\mathrm e$ is called the natural logarithm, denoted $\ln N$.

> **Common Conclusion 4.3.3** (Relationship between Logarithms and Exponents) <br>$a^{\log_a N}=N$, $\log_a a^x=x$ ($a>0$, $a\neq 1$, $N>0$).

> **Common Conclusion 4.3.4** (Operational Properties of Logarithms) <br>$\log_a(MN)=\log_a M+\log_a N$, $\log_a\frac{M}{N}=\log_a M-\log_a N$, $\log_a M^n=n\log_a M$ ($a>0$, $a\neq 1$, $M>0$, $N>0$).

> **Common Conclusion 4.3.5** (Change-of-Base Formula for Logarithms) <br>$\log_a b=\frac{\log_c b}{\log_c a}$ ($a>0$, $a\neq 1$, $c>0$, $c\neq 1$, $b>0$).

> **Definition 4.4.1** (Logarithmic Function) <br>In general, the function $y=\log_a x$ ($a>0$, and $a\neq 1$) is called a logarithmic function, with domain $(0,+\infty)$.

> **Definition 4.4.2** (Inverse Function) <br>The exponential function $y=a^x$ ($a>0$, and $a\neq 1$) and the logarithmic function $y=\log_a x$ ($a>0$, and $a\neq 1$) are inverse functions of each other, and their domains and ranges are exactly swapped.

> **Common Conclusion 4.4.3** (Graphs and Properties of Logarithmic Functions) <br>The domain is $(0,+\infty)$, the range is $\mathbb R$, and the graph passes through the fixed point $(1,0)$; it is an increasing function when $a>1$, and a decreasing function when $0<a<1$. The graphs of two logarithmic functions whose bases are reciprocals of each other are symmetric about the $x$-axis.

> **Common Conclusion 4.4.4** (Differences in Growth of Different Functions) <br>The growth rate of the exponential function $y=a^x$ ($a>1$) eventually far exceeds that of the linear function $y=kx$ ($k>0$); the growth rate of the logarithmic function $y=\log_a x$ ($a>1$) becomes slower and slower, eventually slower than that of the linear function $y=kx$ ($k>0$).

> **Definition 4.5.1** (Zeros of a Function) <br>For a general function $y=f(x)$, the real number $x$ that makes $f(x)=0$ is called a zero of the function $y=f(x)$.

> **Common Conclusion 4.5.2** (Existence Theorem for Zeros of a Function) <br>If the graph of the function $y=f(x)$ on the interval $[a,b]$ is a continuous unbroken curve and $f(a)f(b)<0$, then the function $y=f(x)$ has at least one zero in the interval $(a,b)$.

> **Definition 4.5.3** (Bisection Method) <br>For a function $y=f(x)$ whose graph on the interval $[a,b]$ is continuous and unbroken and for which $f(a)f(b)<0$, the method of repeatedly bisecting the interval containing the zero, gradually approaching the zero with the two endpoints of the resulting interval, and thereby obtaining an approximate value of the zero, is called the bisection method.

(1) $\mathrm e^{0.8}$ ___ $0.8^{\mathrm e}$;

(2) $2^{a+1}$ ___ $3^a$ ($a>2$);

(3) $a^{0.2}$ ___ $a^{0.3}$ ($0<a<1$);

(4) $\lg\mathrm e$ ___ $\ln 0.8$;

(5) $\log_2 3$ ___ $\log_3 2$;

(6) $\log_a 0.2$ ___ $\log_a 0.3$ ($a>1$).

### Solution to Problem 2

By **Common Conclusion 4.2.2** (Graphs and Properties of Exponential Functions) and **Common Conclusion 4.4.3** (Graphs and Properties of Logarithmic Functions).

(1) $\mathrm e^{0.8}\approx 2.23$, $0.8^{\mathrm e}\approx 0.48$, hence $\mathrm e^{0.8}>0.8^{\mathrm e}$.

(2) Take $a=3$, $2^{4}=16<27=3^3$, hence $2^{a+1}<3^a$.

(3) When $0<a<1$, $y=a^x$ is decreasing, and $0.2<0.3$, hence $a^{0.2}>a^{0.3}$.

(4) $\lg\mathrm e\approx 0.434>0$, $\ln 0.8<0$, hence $\lg\mathrm e>\ln 0.8$.

(5) $\log_2 3>1$, $\log_3 2<1$, hence $\log_2 3>\log_3 2$.

(6) When $a>1$, $y=\log_a x$ is increasing, and $0.2<0.3$, hence $\log_a 0.2<\log_a 0.3$. $\blacksquare$

(1) The largest root of the equation $2x^3-4x^2-3x+1=0$ (accuracy $0.01$);

(2) The x-coordinate of the intersection point of the functions $f(x)=\lg x$ and $g(x)=\frac{1}{x}$ (accuracy $0.1$).

### Solution to Problem 3

By **Definition 4.5.3** (Bisection Method).

### Problem 4

(Review Reference Problem 4, Review and Consolidation, Problem 4) Given the function $f(x)=\begin{cases}x^2+2x-3, & x\le 0,\\ -2+\ln x, & x>0,\end{cases}$ find the corresponding range of $k$ for which the equation $f(x)=k$ has $1$, $2$, and $3$ real solutions respectively.

### Solution to Problem 4

By **Definition 4.5.1** (Zeros of a Function) and **Common Conclusion 4.4.3** (Graphs and Properties of Logarithmic Functions).

When $x\le 0$, $y=(x+1)^2-4$, with minimum $-4$ (at $x=-1$), and at $x=0$, $y=-3$. When $x>0$, $y=-2+\ln x$ is an increasing function, with exactly one solution $x=\mathrm e^{k+2}$ for any $k$.

When $k<-4$, the $x\le 0$ part has no solution, for a total of $1$ solution; when $k=-4$, the $x\le 0$ part has $1$ solution $x=-1$, for a total of $2$ solutions; when $-4<k\le -3$, the $x\le 0$ part has $2$ solutions, for a total of $3$ solutions; when $k>-3$, the $x\le 0$ part has $1$ solution, for a total of $2$ solutions.

Hence for $1$ solution, $k<-4$; for $2$ solutions, $k=-4$ or $k>-3$; for $3$ solutions, $-4<k\le -3$. $\blacksquare$

### Problem 6

(Review Reference Problem 4, Comprehensive Application, Problem 6) Let $f(x)=\frac{\mathrm e^x-\mathrm e^{-x}}{2}$, $g(x)=\frac{\mathrm e^x+\mathrm e^{-x}}{2}$. Prove:

(1) $[g(x)]^2-[f(x)]^2=1$;

(2) $f(2x)=2f(x)g(x)$;

(3) $g(2x)=[g(x)]^2+[f(x)]^2$.

### Solution to Problem 6

By **Common Conclusion 4.1.6** (Operational Properties of Real Exponents).

(1) $[g(x)]^2-[f(x)]^2=\frac{(\mathrm e^x+\mathrm e^{-x})^2-(\mathrm e^x-\mathrm e^{-x})^2}{4}=\frac{4\mathrm e^x\mathrm e^{-x}}{4}=1$.

(2) $2f(x)g(x)=2\cdot\frac{\mathrm e^x-\mathrm e^{-x}}{2}\cdot\frac{\mathrm e^x+\mathrm e^{-x}}{2}=\frac{\mathrm e^{2x}-\mathrm e^{-2x}}{2}=f(2x)$.

(3) $[g(x)]^2+[f(x)]^2=\frac{(\mathrm e^x+\mathrm e^{-x})^2+(\mathrm e^x-\mathrm e^{-x})^2}{4}=\frac{2(\mathrm e^{2x}+\mathrm e^{-2x})}{4}=\frac{\mathrm e^{2x}+\mathrm e^{-2x}}{2}=g(2x)$. $\blacksquare$

### Problem 8

(Review Reference Problem 4, Comprehensive Application, Problem 8) In 1986 the Chernobyl nuclear power plant exploded; strontium-90, the main nuclear pollutant, decays at an annual rate of $2.47\%$. To completely eliminate the impact of this nuclear accident on the natural environment requires at least $800$ years; by then, what percentage of the original strontium-90 remains?

### Solution to Problem 8

By **Common Conclusion 4.2.3** (Exponential Growth and Decay Models).

The annual decay rate is $2.47\%$, i.e. $97.53\%$ remains each year. After $800$ years the remaining fraction is $0.9753^{800}=\mathrm e^{800\ln 0.9753}\approx\mathrm e^{800\times(-0.0250)}\approx\mathrm e^{-20}\approx 2\times 10^{-7}\%$. i.e. by then about $2\times 10^{-7}\%$ of the original strontium-90 remains. $\blacksquare$

### Problem 9

(Review Reference Problem 4, Comprehensive Application, Problem 9) For a factory's waste gas, the pollutant content $P$ (unit: $\mathrm{mg/L}$) as a function of time $t$ (unit: $\mathrm h$) is $P=P_0\mathrm e^{-kt}$, where $P_0$ and $k$ are positive constants. In the first $5\;\mathrm h$, $10\%$ of the pollutant is removed.

(1) After $10\;\mathrm h$, what percentage of the pollutant remains?

(2) How long does it take for the pollutant to decrease by $50\%$ (accurate to $1\;\mathrm h$)?

(3) Sketch the graph of $P$ as a function of $t$.

![week29-2.svg](../../figures/week29-2.svg){width=50%}

### Solution to Problem 9

By **Common Conclusion 4.2.3** (Exponential Growth and Decay Models) and **Common Conclusion 4.3.3** (Relationship between Logarithms and Exponents).

(1) $P(5)=P_0\mathrm e^{-5k}=0.9P_0$, i.e. $\mathrm e^{-5k}=0.9$. $P(10)=P_0\mathrm e^{-10k}=P_0(\mathrm e^{-5k})^2=0.81P_0$. Hence after $10\;\mathrm h$, $81\%$ of the pollutant remains.

(2) Set $P_0\mathrm e^{-kt}=0.5P_0$, i.e. $\mathrm e^{-kt}=0.5$, $t=-\frac{\ln 0.5}{k}$. From $\mathrm e^{-5k}=0.9$ we get $k=-\frac{\ln 0.9}{5}\approx 0.021$. Hence $t=\frac{\ln 2}{0.021}\approx 33\;\mathrm h$.

(3) $P=P_0\mathrm e^{-kt}$ is a decreasing exponential decay curve, gradually approaching $0$ from $P_0$. $\blacksquare$

### Problem 10

(Review Reference Problem 4, Comprehensive Application, Problem 10) The cooling formula for an object is $\theta=\theta_0+(\theta_1-\theta_0)\mathrm e^{-kt}$. Now an object at $62{}^\circ\mathrm C$ is placed in air at $15{}^\circ\mathrm C$ to cool; after $1\;\mathrm{min}$ the object's temperature is $52{}^\circ\mathrm C$.

(1) Find the value of $k$ (accurate to $0.01$);

(2) To lower the object's temperature to $42{}^\circ\mathrm C$ and $32{}^\circ\mathrm C$, find the required cooling times respectively (accurate to $0.1\;\mathrm{min}$).

### Solution to Problem 10

By **Common Conclusion 4.3.3** (Relationship between Logarithms and Exponents).

(1) Substituting $\theta=52$, $\theta_0=15$, $\theta_1=62$, $t=1$: $52=15+47\mathrm e^{-k}$, $\mathrm e^{-k}=\frac{37}{47}$, $k=-\ln\frac{37}{47}\approx 0.24$.

(2) Set $\theta=42$: $42=15+47\mathrm e^{-0.24t}$, $\mathrm e^{-0.24t}=\frac{27}{47}$, $t=\frac{-\ln(27/47)}{0.24}\approx 2.3\;\mathrm{min}$. Set $\theta=32$: $32=15+47\mathrm e^{-0.24t}$, $\mathrm e^{-0.24t}=\frac{17}{47}$, $t=\frac{-\ln(17/47)}{0.24}\approx 4.5\;\mathrm{min}$. $\blacksquare$

### Problem 11

(Review Reference Problem 4, Extension and Exploration, Problem 11) Given the functions $f(x)=\log_a(x+1)$, $g(x)=\log_a(1-x)$ ($a>0$, and $a\neq 1$).

(1) Find the domain of the function $f(x)+g(x)$;

(2) Determine the odd-even property of the function $f(x)+g(x)$, and explain why.

### Solution to Problem 11

By **Definition 4.4.1** (Logarithmic Function).

(1) We require $x+1>0$ and $1-x>0$, i.e. $-1<x<1$. Hence the domain is $(-1,1)$.

(2) $F(x)=f(x)+g(x)=\log_a(x+1)+\log_a(1-x)=\log_a(1-x^2)$. $F(-x)=\log_a(1-(-x)^2)=\log_a(1-x^2)=F(x)$. Hence $F(x)$ is an even function. $\blacksquare$

### Problem 12

(Review Reference Problem 4, Extension and Exploration, Problem 12) For the function $f(x)=a-\frac{2}{2^x+1}$ ($a\in\mathbb R$).

(1) Explore the monotonicity of the function $f(x)$;

(2) Does there exist a real number $a$ such that the function $f(x)$ is an odd function?

### Solution to Problem 12

By **Common Conclusion 4.2.2** (Graphs and Properties of Exponential Functions).

(1) $2^x$ is an increasing function, hence $\frac{2}{2^x+1}$ is a decreasing function and $-\frac{2}{2^x+1}$ is an increasing function; therefore $f(x)=a-\frac{2}{2^x+1}$ is an increasing function.

(2) If $f(x)$ is an odd function, then $f(0)=0$, i.e. $a-\frac{2}{2}=0$, $a=1$. Verify: $f(x)=1-\frac{2}{2^x+1}=\frac{2^x-1}{2^x+1}$. $f(-x)=\frac{2^{-x}-1}{2^{-x}+1}=\frac{1-2^x}{1+2^x}=-f(x)$. Hence there exists $a=1$ making $f(x)$ an odd function. $\blacksquare$

### Problem 13

(Review Reference Problem 4, Extension and Exploration, Problem 13) The graph of the function $y=f(x)$ consists of the curve segment $OA$ and the line segment $AB$, where $O(0,0)$, $A(2,3)$, $B(5,0)$.

(1) Write an analytic expression for the function $y=f(x)$;

(2) Pose a practical problem that satisfies the variation pattern of the graph of the function $y=f(x)$.

### Solution to Problem 13

By **Common Conclusion 4.4.4** (Differences in Growth of Different Functions).

(2) A practical problem: for example, an object first undergoes accelerated motion to reach its highest point, then undergoes uniform decelerated motion to return to its initial position. $\blacksquare$
